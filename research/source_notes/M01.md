## Methodological procedure established from available search snippets

Full text was not directly retrievable (ScienceDirect is a blocked domain in this environment); the following is reconstructed from multiple converging secondary summaries (ResearchGate abstract page, Studocu study notes, IDEAS/RePEc listing, and AI-generated search synopses of the article).

**1. Typology of review approaches.** Snyder (2019) distinguishes three broad literature-review methodologies, each suited to a different purpose:
- **Systematic review** — aims at a (typically quantitative) synthesis and comparison of prior evidence on a narrowly defined research question, following explicit, replicable search and selection procedures.
- **Semi-systematic (or "narrative") review** — appropriate when the literature on a topic is diverse and has been studied by researchers across different disciplines, aiming to provide an overview of how a research field or topic has developed over time; combines some systematic rigor with more flexible, narrative synthesis.
- **Integrative (or critical) review** — aims to critique and synthesize prior perspectives in order to reconceptualize or extend theory, often generating a new conceptual framework rather than merely summarizing prior findings.

This typology is directly relevant to the thesis's Methodology chapter insofar as it can be used to justify why a systematic-review design (rather than a purely narrative or integrative one) was chosen for a bounded question such as "barriers to reshoring from Asian markets."

**2. Four-stage review process.** Multiple secondary summaries describe Snyder's recommended review process as consisting of four sequential stages:
1. **Design** — formulating the review question(s) and deciding which review approach (systematic, semi-systematic, integrative) fits the purpose; planning the overall review protocol.
2. **Conduct** (search and selection) — searching for literature (e.g., in bibliographic databases), screening records against inclusion/exclusion criteria, and documenting the selection process transparently; one summary specifically notes that texts should be screened in full to confirm they meet inclusion criteria, and that reference lists of selected articles can be hand-searched ("snowballing") to identify further relevant literature, with the inclusion/exclusion decisions documented carefully.
3. **Analysis** — critically analyzing and examining the identified literature, extracting main ideas/relationships, and (for an integrative review specifically) requiring more advanced conceptual/interpretive work than a purely descriptive summary, while remaining transparent about the analytical process.
4. **Writing up / reporting** — presenting the review in a way that goes beyond a mere overview or description; for integrative reviews in particular, Snyder emphasizes that the output should advance knowledge or theory, ideally proposing a new conceptual framework or research agenda.

**Confidence note:** the four-stage labels ("design," "conduct," "analysis," "writing up") are consistently reproduced across independent secondary sources (Studocu notes, AI search synopses), which increases confidence, but the exact wording Snyder uses for each stage, and any sub-steps within each stage, have not been confirmed against the primary PDF. 1. Systematic review — for a sufficiently specific question, aiming to identify the full relevant body of literature using pre-established inclusion/exclusion criteria, in a transparent and reproducible process. The docx identifies this as the thesis's own primary approach.

The docx notes the thesis's evidence is heterogeneous but the research question is specific enough that "systematic literature review" remains the correct label rather than "semi-systematic review."
3. Integrative review — oriented toward combining different theoretical strands into a new conceptual model.
