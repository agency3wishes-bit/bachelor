## Methodological procedure established from available search snippets

The article was published in an open-access journal (BMC Medical Research Methodology); a PMC (PubMed Central) copy exists (PMC2478656), and a City University open-access repository copy is also indexed, though direct fetch of publisher/repository domains was not attempted per the task's network constraint — the following is based on WebSearch summaries of these open-access copies, which is a comparatively reliable secondary route for this particular source since PMC abstracts/summaries closely mirror the primary text.

**Three-step thematic synthesis procedure**, consistent with the corpus classification's own description ("line-by-line coding, descriptive themes and analytical themes"):

1. **Line-by-line coding of the findings of primary studies.** The text of included studies (specifically their reported findings/results sections) is coded line by line, similarly to how a single qualitative primary study would be coded — codes capture the meaning and content of sentences/statements across the set of studies being synthesized.
2. **Development of descriptive themes.** Codes are then organized and grouped into descriptive themes. This step remains "close" to the primary studies — i.e., descriptive themes are essentially a structured, higher-order organization of what the primary studies already report, without adding interpretation beyond the source material.
3. **Generation of analytical themes.** From the descriptive themes, the reviewers generate analytical themes, which represent a further stage of interpretation. At this stage the reviewers "go beyond" the content of the primary studies to produce new interpretive constructs, explanations, or hypotheses that were not explicitly stated in any single included study but emerge from synthesizing across the set.

This descriptive-then-analytical-themes distinction is the core contribution most consistently cited when Thomas & Harden (2008) is referenced in later methodology literature, and is corroborated by multiple independent search summaries in this search round (converging on identical language: "line-by-line coding," "descriptive themes... close to the primary studies," "analytical themes... go beyond the primary studies").

**Stated rationale (per search summaries):** thematic synthesis is presented by the authors as a method that preserves an explicit and transparent, auditable link between the review's conclusions and the original text of the primary studies — i.e., a reviewer or reader can trace an analytical theme back through the descriptive themes to the specific coded text passages in the primary studies. This transparency/traceability rationale is offered as a strength relative to less structured qualitative synthesis approaches, positioning thematic synthesis as compatible with the rigor expectations of systematic reviewing while still permitting the kind of interpretive theory-building associated with qualitative research synthesis.

**Concrete methodological procedure described (extends the existing three-step summary with a worked example directly adapted to this thesis):**

1. **Line-by-line coding.** The document adapts this for the thesis's heterogeneous corpus: the coding unit need not be a literal line, but any "meaningful evidence segment" — e.g., a sentence from a Results section, an interview quotation, a description of an implementation challenge, a survey finding, or a Discussion paragraph clearly grounded in the study's own results. Worked example given in the document: the evidence sentence "It took us about 12 months to buy the machine, learn how to use it and train the person" is coded with initial codes *machinery acquisition*, *workforce training*, *operational learning curve*, *implementation delay*, *ramp-up difficulty*.
2. **Descriptive themes.** Similar initial codes are grouped; e.g., "shortage of technicians" and "loss of tacit knowledge" → descriptive theme *Skilled-labour shortage*; "need to retrain workers" and "long machine-learning period" → descriptive theme *Workforce capability rebuilding*. These two descriptive themes can be further grouped under a broader descriptive theme, *Domestic workforce and competence gaps*.
3. **Analytical themes.** The document illustrates this as the interpretive step where the reviewer goes beyond restating study content, e.g.: "Skills shortages, tacit-knowledge loss, machinery-learning requirements and absent supplier know-how indicate that reshoring is a process of domestic capability reconstruction rather than a simple reversal of offshoring" — explicitly framed as directly addressing the core RQ and Sub-RQ1.

**Hybrid deductive–inductive adaptation recommended for this thesis:** the four overarching categories (Regulatory/Institutional, Cultural/Organizational, Cost/Operational, Asset/Capability) are set **deductively** from the theoretical framework and research questions, while specific barrier codes and subthemes are developed **inductively** from the included studies; category boundaries may be refined after coding, and overlapping barriers can receive a primary and a secondary category. A traceability chain is recommended for each barrier: *Original evidence → Initial code → Descriptive theme → Analytical category → Theoretical interpretation → Managerial implication* (worked example: "Firm needed 12 months to acquire machinery and train staff" → codes: machinery acquisition, training, learning curve, delay → descriptive theme: Domestic production capability rebuilding → category: Asset/Capability → theory: RBV/Dynamic Capabilities → managerial implication: conduct capability audit and plan staged ramp-up).

**Suggested Methodology wording (verbatim from the document, offered as a drafting aid, not a source quotation):** "The extracted evidence was synthesised using an adapted thematic synthesis approach based on Thomas and Harden (2008). First, relevant findings were coded at the level of meaningful evidence segments. Second, conceptually similar codes were grouped into descriptive barrier themes. Third, the descriptive themes were interpreted to generate higher-order analytical themes addressing the research questions. The analysis followed a hybrid deductive–inductive logic..."

**Important corrective note found in the document itself:** an earlier draft in the same working conversation had called Thomas & Harden a "gold standard," which the document explicitly retracts as too strong. Corrected framing: the method is authoritative and widely used, but (a) it was originally developed for systematic reviews of **qualitative** research, (b) its worked example is drawn from **public health**, (c) this thesis's corpus is heterogeneous (qualitative case studies, quantitative surveys, systematic reviews, conceptual papers, macro studies) rather than a uniform qualitative dataset, and (d) it does not replace search, screening, PRISMA reporting, or quality-appraisal procedures. Recommended phrasing: "Thomas and Harden (2008) provide a transparent three-stage procedure for synthesising qualitative findings across studies and developing higher-order analytical themes," combined with an explicit note that "the methodological design and evidence level of each source were retained as separate coding dimensions" so that, e.g., a survey result and an interview quotation supporting the same theme are not treated as equivalent evidence types.

**Comparison to Braun & Clarke (mentioned in the document only for methodological justification, not as a corpus source):** the document argues Thomas & Harden is the better fit for this thesis because it is designed for synthesis **across** multiple studies (a systematic review), whereas Braun & Clarke's thematic analysis is designed for a single qualitative dataset (e.g., one set of interviews or documents). Also mentioned only as context (not as a source to be added): Tranfield et al. is referenced in the document as a desirable source for "systematic review in management" design — **this is not part of the approved 33-source corpus and must not be cited in the thesis.**
