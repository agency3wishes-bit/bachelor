## What could be established from available search snippets
Confidence: **abstract-level and indexed-summary level only.** Full text not retrieved (Springer blocked; a CentAUR open-access copy exists but was not fetched).

The article is a **conceptual/theoretical contribution**, consistent with the corpus classification. Its stated aim is to offer "a conceptual framework to understand why firms reshore and why such decisions remain relatively rare" (Springer/RePEc abstract, surfaced by the title + author query). The framing: as the costs of global production fragmentation rise, advanced economies have introduced policies promoting relocation of previously offshored tasks back to the home country, encouraging investment in automation and green technologies.

The core conceptual logic surfaced consistently across the Springer abstract and IDEAS/EconPapers snippets:
- Value chains are **"sticky," particularly for large firms**, and investment in automation **implies new (fixed) costs** for firms already burdened by rising offshoring costs.
- For **larger firms**, automation-driven reshoring may **not be as profitable** as alternatives such as nearshoring or friendshoring.
- **Smaller and less internationally exposed firms** may turn to reshoring to contain losses — "ultimately, to survive" (this is the paper's titular "survival reshoring" argument).
- The framework draws on **Antràs's** work on de-globalization / GVCs in the post-COVID-19 age; one snippet paraphrases the mechanism as automation being "conceptualized as an increase in domestic fixed costs that allows for marginal cost reductions" (attributed in the snippet to Antràs 2020, i.e. a cited source within the paper, not an original finding).

**Important disambiguation (flagged):** A search snippet referenced **D'Ambrosio et al. (2024)** developing a methodology to measure reshoring from balance-sheet and trade administrative data and identifying **1,559 reshoring firms in Italy over 2009–2015.** That is a *different, earlier* paper by (partly) the same author, **not** A16. A16 itself is conceptual with no primary empirical data. Do **not** attribute the "1,559 Italian firms" figure to A16.

**Confirms the core classification.** The docx confirms A16 is a **conceptual/theoretical article based on literature and secondary evidence, with no primary empirical data**, addressing why reshoring remains rare despite growing de-globalization pressures. It explicitly corrects an initial NotebookLM overstatement — "the article answers the research question directly and provides theoretical and empirical evidence" — clarifying instead: "the article answers the research question directly at a conceptual level, using existing research, surveys, and secondary evidence, but does not provide its own firm-level observations of implementation barriers." This matches and reinforces the existing note's "conceptual, PARTIAL" framing throughout — no contradiction.

**Explicit theoretical base — adds a correction/sharpening not present in the existing note.** The docx states that the article's own main theoretical basis is the **Antràs framework** (fixed costs, firm heterogeneity, offshoring decisions, automation, irreversibility, GVC stickiness) — described as the "explicit theoretical core" of the paper. TCE and RBV are explicitly framed as *supplementary interpretive lenses the docx author would apply*, not confirmed as frameworks the paper itself develops in depth: "TCE and RBV should be used as additional interpretation, if the article itself does not develop them in detail." This is a useful sharpening of the existing note's Gap #5 (whether the paper uses TCE/RBV) — the docx suggests Antràs (2020)-style de-globalization/GVC theory is the safer primary attribution for the thesis's Literature Review, with TCE/RBV positioned as the thesis's own interpretive add-on rather than the paper's stated lens.

**Barriers identified (per docx), all explicitly labeled non-empirical ("Conceptual" or "Review/conceptual"), consistent with the existing note's PARTIAL verification status:**
1. **RESHORING_NEW_FIXED_COSTS** (Cost/Operational; Planning→Implementation) — building/restoring a plant, purchasing equipment, automation, hiring/training workers, building a domestic supplier network, organizing new production processes. Described as one of the most direct implementation barriers in the article.
2. **GVC_NETWORK_STICKINESS** (Asset/Capability + Cost/Operational; Exit→Transfer→Reintegration) — arising from sunk investment in offshore production, accumulated supplier relationships, specialized infrastructure, location-specific knowledge, GVC interdependence, and the difficulty of reproducing the network at home.
3. **DOMESTIC_SKILLED_WORKFORCE_SHORTAGE** (Asset/Capability + Human Resources; Reintegration/ramp-up) — reinforces the cluster already supported by A04, A06, A09, A11, and now (per the A13 supplementary note above) A13.
4. **HOME_COUNTRY_FACTOR_COST_DISADVANTAGE** (Cost/Operational; Decision→Implementation viability) — wages, energy, land, regulatory compliance; framed by the docx as more a barrier to economic feasibility/scale than to physical relocation itself.
5. **RESHORING_MANAGERIAL_CAPABILITY_GAP** (Cultural/Organizational + Asset/Capability; Evaluation→Planning→Implementation) — particularly relevant to SMEs lacking evaluation methods, international experience, managerial resources, automation-implementation skills, and coordination capacity for a complex relocation programme.
6. **AUTOMATION_SCALE_THRESHOLD** (Cost/Operational + Asset/Capability; Planning/implementation viability) — automation does not automatically ease reshoring; requires sufficient production volume, substantial upfront investment, technical competencies, and complementary assets. Docx notes this complements A07 and A12.
7. **RESHORING_RETURN_UNCERTAINTY** (Cost/Operational + Cultural/Organizational; Decision/Planning) — uncertainty about future offshore costs, domestic operating costs, technology returns, demand, and geopolitical developments; framed primarily as a decision/planning-stage barrier.

The docx is explicit that none of these seven codes should increase the count of *direct empirical* barrier confirmations in the thesis's synthesis — they remain conceptual.

**Sunk costs vs. new fixed costs — an important conceptual distinction added by the docx, not present in the existing note.** The docx draws a careful line between (a) already-incurred, unrecoverable **offshore sunk costs** (foreign plant construction, worker training, supplier development, contracts, foreign network development) — which explain inertia/GVC stickiness but are not themselves new implementation costs — and (b) **new fixed costs of reshoring** (domestic factory, equipment, training, supplier reconstruction, automation, transition) — which are the actual cost barrier to implementation. The thesis should preserve this distinction when discussing A16's cost arguments, to avoid conflating "why firms don't leave" with "what it costs to return."

**Survival reshoring vs. strategic reshoring typology — new content, useful for Discussion.** The docx confirms and elaborates the "survival reshoring" concept: reshoring is not confined to technologically advanced, high-productivity, large firms pursuing automation/innovation/upgrading ("strategic/virtuous reshoring"); it can also be undertaken by smaller or less productive firms for whom offshore operations become too complex or costly, as a way to reduce losses, simplify configuration, and cope with an inability to sustain the offshore network ("survival reshoring"). The docx flags this as a conceptual typology, not an empirically observed sequence in the paper's own data.

**Asia relevance — CONFIRMS the existing note's caution, with more explicit detail and an explicit correct-citation-language warning.** The docx confirms the paper discusses China, decoupling, changing costs in emerging Asian economies, Vietnam, Indonesia, Bangladesh, and geopolitical tensions, but explicitly labels this **macroeconomic/conceptual context**, not firm-level empirical findings. The docx gives an explicit worked example of what NOT to write: "D'Ambrosio and Lavoratori empirically found that firms leaving China faced skilled-labour shortages" is flagged as incorrect; the correct formulation given is: "D'Ambrosio and Lavoratori (2025) conceptually explain why the reversal of offshore production may be constrained by new fixed costs, limited domestic skills and the embeddedness of firms in existing global value chains." This citation-language guidance should be applied directly in the thesis text.
