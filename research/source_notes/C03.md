## What could be established from available search snippets
Confidence: moderate — two independent search rounds converged on the same methodological description and a consistent (though not independently cross-checked) set of empirical results.

- The paper proposes a **new measure of global industry relocation using inter-country input-output tables**, decomposing industry relocation into three components: **(i) relocation driven by intermediate inputs, (ii) relocation driven by final products, and (iii) indirect intermediate relocation driven by final products.** The unit of analysis is explicitly **country–industry flows**, not individual firms — consistent with the corpus's own framing.
- The study covers the period **2007–2014** and treats global manufacturing location as **the dynamic net result of three competing/co-occurring relocation patterns**: offshoring, re-shoring, and re-offshoring (i.e., a good can be offshored, then re-shored back to the original country, or re-offshored onward to a third country).
- **Reported empirical patterns (per one converged search summary):**
  1. Re-shoring is **more likely in capital- or technology-intensive manufacturing** than in labour-intensive manufacturing.
  2. Manufacturing production previously offshored **to high-income economies** is described as **"much more locationally flexible"** (i.e., more prone to further movement — either re-shoring or re-offshoring — than production offshored to lower-income economies).
  3. Re-shoring, **especially in labour-intensive industries**, is **more likely between countries with a higher degree of geographic/economic proximity**.
- **Country-level "re-shoring magnitude" percentages** were reported in one search summary: Europe ~27%, China ~40%, USA ~63%, UK ~47%, South Korea ~60% (Germany mentioned but no percentage captured). **These figures are UNVERIFIED as to their precise definition** — it is not clear from the search snippet alone whether "re-shoring magnitude" means the share of a country's total manufacturing relocation flows that is re-shoring (as opposed to offshoring/re-offshoring), the share of previously offshored output that returned, or some other index constructed by the paper's methodology. This must not be quoted in the thesis as a plain "percentage of firms reshoring" without reading the primary tables, since doing so would risk a serious construct-validity misrepresentation (macro input-output flow share vs. firm-level reshoring incidence are very different things).

## Explicit boundary check
This source does **not** provide firm-level reshoring implementation evidence and is **not**, by itself, a study of "genuine reshoring" in the thesis's strict sense (Asian host to home-country return, observed at firm level). It is a **macro, country–industry input-output flow study** that measures net relocation direction (offshoring vs. re-shoring vs. re-offshoring) across the global economy for the 2007–2014 period. Some of the countries in its results (e.g., China, South Korea) are Asian economies appearing as **origins or destinations of relocation flows** within a global system — this is what gives the source its "Medium" Asia specificity in the corpus — but the paper does not isolate or report a specific Asia-to-home-country firm-level reshoring case or process. It is therefore usable only as **macro background** (Introduction/Discussion) to characterize the scale and sectoral/proximity patterns of global relocation flows, and explicitly must not be used as direct or even review-level evidence for implementation barriers, consistent with the corpus's "No — macro patterns only" classification.

Their exclusion from the thesis is now positively confirmed as correct, not merely precautionary. Unit of analysis (country–industry flows) confirmed; context-only classification stands.
